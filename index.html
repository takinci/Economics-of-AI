Who pays for radiology AI?

A lot of money has gone into radiology AI. Very little of its value has been measured, and even less is billed. This briefing follows the money on a world tour of 18 stops on seven continents, from New Zealand to Washington, and asks who pays for AI and who keeps the value it creates.

What AI gives back

Value comes in three forms

Where value has been measured, it shows up as money, time or safety. Only 6 of 31 economic evaluations of radiology AI measured productivity, even though saving time is a central argument for AI (Gregory et al., Eur Radiol 2026).

Separate payment for AI?

The global picture

A separate fee for radiology AI is the exception

Of 18 stops, 3 have a separate fee: the United States, where insurers pay through billing codes, and Korea and Taiwan, where the patient pays most or all of it. Japan and Germany pay indirectly, inside another payment. Kazakhstan’s code is only reported. Everywhere else, the answer is no: the money comes another way, from the state, the hospital or a donor.

Much of the world was not covered by our tour. When it comes to paying for radiology AI, it is still uncharted territory.

The proof problem

Few studies, mostly modelled, and almost all favourable

Payers move carefully because the evidence is thin. Radiology cost studies almost all favour AI, but they are modelled, not tested in real patients. When almost every result is positive, publication bias is likely.

What decides payment

Evidence shapes whether AI is paid for; where the value lands shapes how

Outside the United States, insurers rarely pay a separate fee for AI. What matters is whether the tool is attached to new money.

Value is efficiency (time, workload)
Value is a new clinical result
Weak or modelled evidence

Stronger evidence, or new programme money

United States: the insurer pays per usePer-use codes, e.g. AI plaque analysis (CPT 75577) and a stroke-triage add-on payment

The precedent

2002: how paying per use ended

When Medicare began paying separately for mammography computer-aided detection, use soared without improving accuracy. Medicare later folded the separate payment into the screening fee. It is a cautionary tale for any payer considering a per-use fee.

<5% → 74%of Medicare screening mammograms read with CAD, 2001 to 2008Lobig et al., npj Digit Med 2023
~$400Madded spending per year, with no real-world gain in accuracyLobig et al. 2023, citing Lehman et al., JAMA Intern Med 2015

What to do

Recommendations

If you buy AI

From our systematic review (Molwitz et al., Radiol Artif Intell 2026), with examples from the tour.

  1. Target real bottlenecksLung screening in Germany; mammography in one Danish region
  2. Fixed price beats pay-per-useCentral buyers license at scale; TB programmes can buy unlimited-read licences
  3. Monitor real-world outcomesKorea’s conditional use and NICE’s evidence-generation period allow use while evidence accrues
  4. Look beyond diagnosisEvery per-use payment we found is for image analysis; workflow AI must pay for itself

If you pay for AI

Lessons for payers, drawn from the 18 stops.

  1. Measure value in all three formsMoney, time and safety, so it is clear who benefits
  2. Reserve per-use payment for new clinical resultsFund efficiency tools through programmes or budgets
  3. Attach payment to high-volume programmesAs Germany did with lung screening, and align pricing with volume
  4. Link payment to real-world monitoringOf clinical and economic outcomes, with a defined point for review
